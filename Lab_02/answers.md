## Questions to Answer

**Which three pretrained models performed best in Lab Activity 1?**
The three models carried forward into this lab are VGG16, ResNet18, and EfficientNet-B0. (This answer assumes these are the three Task 01 identified as best — update this line if Task 01's ranking named different models.)

**How does filtering affect each of the three models?**
- **VGG16** is the most filter-sensitive in a *positive* direction: Average and Sharpening both raise accuracy and macro-F1 well above baseline (Average: 77.68% acc / 49.02% F1 vs. 74.14% / 38.77% baseline), and Gaussian/Median also help.
- **ResNet18** is largely unaffected or slightly hurt by the smoothing filters (Average, Gaussian) — accuracy and F1 stay close to or below baseline — with Median and Sharpening giving small F1 gains.
- **EfficientNet-B0** sees small, fairly consistent gains from Average/Gaussian/Median/Sharpening over baseline, but the gains are smaller than VGG16's.
- All three models degrade sharply under **Sobel**.

**Which filter produces the greatest change compared with the unfiltered baseline?**
**Sobel**, by a wide margin — it's the only filter that *hurts* every model on every metric (e.g. VGG16 accuracy drops from 74.14% to 66.55%, AUC from 90.13% to 80.51%; ResNet18 macro-F1 nearly halves, from 38.00% to 24.28%). Among filters that help, **Average** produces the largest positive change, for VGG16 specifically.

**Does the effect of a filter remain consistent across all three models?**
No, not in magnitude, though Sobel is consistently *negative* in direction for all three. Average, for example, is the best filter for VGG16 (+3.5 accuracy points) but is neutral-to-slightly-negative for ResNet18 (-0.1 points) and moderately positive for EfficientNet-B0 (+1.2 points). So filtering's effect depends on the model architecture, not just the filter.

**Does filtering improve or decrease macro-F1 and balanced accuracy?**
Macro-F1: the four smoothing/sharpening filters (Average, Gaussian, Median, Sharpening) improve macro-F1 for VGG16 and EfficientNet-B0, are roughly flat-to-mixed for ResNet18, and Sobel decreases macro-F1 for all three models substantially. **Why might smoothing remove useful lesion texture or morphological information?**
Average, Gaussian, and Median filters are low-pass: they average out high-frequency spatial detail. In dermoscopic images that detail carries diagnostically relevant texture — pigment network patterns, dot/globule structure, irregular borders, fine vascular structures — so smoothing can blur exactly the morphological cues a classifier (or a dermatologist) relies on, even though it also suppresses noise that might otherwise confuse the model.

**Why might sharpening or edge detection help or hurt classification?**
Sharpening amplifies existing high-frequency detail (edges, texture boundaries), which can help by making lesion borders and texture more prominent — consistent with Sharpening giving VGG16 and EfficientNet-B0 a boost here. Sobel, however, converts the image to gradient-magnitude edges and (in this implementation) collapses it toward a single-channel edge map before replicating it across RGB — discarding color and shading information entirely. Since color (redness, pigmentation, blue-white veil, etc.) is one of the most diagnostically important cues in dermoscopy, this explains Sobel's large performance drop across all three models.

**What is the difference between convolution and correlation?**
Cross-correlation slides a kernel over the image and computes a weighted sum directly with the kernel as given. Convolution does the same but first flips the kernel 180° (both horizontally and vertically) before sliding it. For symmetric kernels (e.g. the Gaussian, mean/box, and the symmetric sharpening kernel used here) convolution and correlation give identical results; for asymmetric kernels (e.g. the Sobel operator) they produce different, mirrored outputs. In practice, most deep-learning frameworks (including PyTorch's `Conv2d`) implement cross-correlation but call it "convolution."

**Based on your results, explain the relationship between classical image processing and deep-learning-based feature extraction.**
Classical filters and CNN convolutional layers are the same mathematical operation — a kernel sliding over an image computing weighted sums — but classical filters are hand-designed to extract one fixed, interpretable property (blur, edges, sharpness), while a CNN backbone learns its own bank of filters end-to-end for whatever features best separate the classes. The results show these two things interact rather than being independent: a hand-designed pre-filter changes what a pretrained CNN "sees" before its own learned filters ever run, so results depend on whether the hand-designed transformation preserves the cues the network's *pretrained* (ImageNet) filters were tuned to detect. Mild smoothing/sharpening filters preserved enough structure to be roughly neutral-to-helpful, while an aggressive, information-discarding transform like Sobel destroyed cues the pretrained backbone relied on (notably color), causing a much larger performance drop than any of the other filters — showing that classical preprocessing is not "free" when feeding a pretrained deep network; it can help or actively conflict with what the network already learned to use.
